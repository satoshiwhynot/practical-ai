# One-person creative team: working kit

Use this with “How to Become a One-Person Creative Team With AI.” Choose your own publishing or client work as the primary route this month. The templates support both routes. Fill them with real source material.

## 2. Questions that develop the point of view

- Which problem have you solved often enough to notice where the usual advice breaks?
- What decision did you change your mind about after doing the work?
- What would you tell a beginner to stop optimizing, and what should they try instead?
- What do people in your field treat as obvious that you think needs evidence?
- Which tradeoff are you willing to defend, and when would you choose differently?
- What can you show: a document, a demonstration or a result you have permission to discuss?
- Whose ideas influenced yours, and what did you change or reject?
- Who do you want to help become more capable, and what should following your work help them do?

## 3. Questions for a source interview

Use these with a client, or answer them from your own experience when relevant.

- What do customers keep asking before they buy?
- Which recent decision would be useful to explain to someone less experienced?
- What advice in your field do you disagree with, and what happened that changed your mind?
- What can we demonstrate with a document, example or product walkthrough?
- Which details must remain private?
- What would you refuse to put your name on, even if it attracted attention?

## 4. Material to collect

- Notes or an interview transcript with source locations and permission to use it.
- Approved writing samples and one disliked sample, with an explanation.
- Documentation and demonstrations relevant to the reader's question.
- Public references with working links and publication dates.
- The intended next step for the reader and the evidence it needs.
- For client work: scope, review deadline and the named approver.

## 5. Five prompts and a calculation

The prompts use “client” for the person whose expertise is being published. For your own brand, that person is you. Develop the point of view, collect the material and prepare the profile and source brief. Use the content plan to choose one idea with enough evidence before drafting. The final block is a calculation.

### Develop a point of view

```text
Help me develop an editorial point of view from the supplied notes,
examples and interview answers. If I am working for a client, use their
material and mark anything that needs their confirmation.

Identify a problem I understand from experience, a belief I can explain,
the experience or evidence behind it, and a situation where it may fail.
Show the source passage for each observation.

Propose two editorial directions. For each, name the reader, the question
I can help them answer, and an example I could actually show.
Do not invent a life story, credentials, results or a provocative opinion.

Ask me one unanswered question at a time before finalizing the brief.
```

### Build the editorial profile

```text
Read the supplied interview and approved writing samples.
Prepare a short editorial profile for a human writer.

Identify the reader, the problem the client helps with, their relevant
experience, and the views they can explain from that experience.
Attach a source passage to each observation.

Describe practical voice choices: sentence length, level of detail,
use of examples, phrases they use, and phrases they reject.
Separate an observed pattern from a question that needs confirmation.
Do not invent a personal story or treat an opinion as a verified result.

Finish with the questions the client needs to answer before drafting.
```

### Prepare the source brief

```text
Using only the supplied interview and documents, prepare an editorial source brief.

Identify the reader's main question and the client's relevant experience.
For each proposed factual claim, attach a source location: a transcript
timestamp, document section or working link.

Keep opinions separate from factual claims. List missing evidence as questions.
Do not invent customer results, quotations, dates, numbers or personal history.

Return the brief and claim ledger before drafting the article.
```

### Plan six pieces

```text
Build a six-piece content plan from the supplied positioning brief,
editorial profile, customer questions and reference material.

For each piece, return:
- The reader's specific question.
- A working title that promises an answer we can support.
- The client's relevant view or experience, with a source location.
- The example, demonstration or evidence the piece needs.
- A suitable format and the useful next step for the reader.
- Missing material and who needs to supply it.

Use references to study how an explanation works. Write original titles
and angles. Do not infer sales from views, likes or bookmarks.
If a linked source is unavailable, ask for its text instead of describing it.
Return a numbered list, not a table. Mark each idea ready or awaiting evidence.
```

### Draft an article for review

```text
Draft an article for [reader] answering [specific question].
Use the attached brief and approved sources. Keep source notes beside factual
claims while drafting. Mark any gap as [EVIDENCE NEEDED].

Develop the strongest example in enough detail for the reader to understand it.
Explain the proposed next step. Use the client's approved writing samples
for guidance on wording and rhythm, without copying their sentences.

Return a draft for human review. Do not publish or schedule it.
```

### Check delivery economics

```text
Fee remaining per delivery hour =
(client fee - direct project costs) / total delivery hours
```

For illustration, a $1,200 fee, $80 of direct project costs and 16 delivery hours leave $70 per delivery hour before overhead and taxes. At 24 hours, the same calculation gives about $46.67. These are hypothetical inputs, not market rates or a revenue promise.

## 6. Review before publishing

- The opening and title promise an answer the article delivers.
- The piece expresses an identifiable view, with its reasoning and limits.
- Factual claims have been checked against the cited material.
- The author has approved quotations, personal details and customer examples.
- A concrete example makes the difficult part understandable.
- Shorter posts each have a separate purpose and can stand alone.
- Links and visual labels work at reader size.
- The piece points to related work where it helps the reader.
- For client work, this version has a named approval and explicit publishing authority.

## 7. Two routes through the first month

The two routes share skills and working documents. Give one priority this month so you have enough time to finish something and assess the response.

### Days 1–7: Define a reader and a point of view

- Own brand: answer the positioning questions, choose a recurring reader problem and gather material for the first piece. Record a view you can explain, its evidence and a counterexample.
- Client work: study one buyer group and prepare a relevant sample from public material. Explain who it helps and why the business might need it.

### Days 8–14: Put a complete piece in front of someone

- Own brand: publish the first substantial piece and one useful supporting post. Give new readers a starting page and a way to respond.
- Client work: show selected prospects the sample and discuss their process. Look for a specific problem they want help solving and agree on a paid pilot if there is a fit.

### Days 15–21: Develop the next piece from the response

- Own brand: use a reader’s question or a gap you found in the first piece to develop a follow-up. Add it to the map and connect the two explanations.
- Client work: start the pilot when scope and source material are agreed. Track the work from interview through review. If there is no fit yet, improve the sample or reconsider the buyer.

### Days 22–30: Decide what deserves to repeat

- Own brand: review the published work and the questions it attracted. Describe one offer you could deliver for those readers, and test whether they want help with it.
- Client work: review completed work, delivery time and the client’s response. Record the next deadline if the pilot is still underway. Use what you learn to narrow the service or define a repeatable arrangement.
